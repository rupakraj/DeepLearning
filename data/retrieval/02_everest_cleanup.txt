Cleanup team brings down eleven tonnes of waste from Everest

A cleanup expedition has returned to Kathmandu with about eleven tonnes of waste collected from the higher camps of Mount Everest, including discarded oxygen cylinders, torn tents, food packaging and abandoned climbing rope.

The team of twenty-two sherpa climbers and soldiers worked for forty-five days between Base Camp and the South Col at 7,900 metres, spending the short weather windows of the spring season carrying loads down instead of pushing for the summit.

Waste that has been frozen into the ice for decades is now surfacing as the glacier thins, expedition leaders said. Some of the oxygen bottles recovered this season carry markings from expeditions of the late 1990s.

Roughly six tonnes of the material is recyclable and has been handed to a recycling enterprise in Kathmandu, where metal cylinders are cut and sold as scrap and old tent fabric is turned into bags sold to visitors.

The expedition also recovered the remains of two climbers who died on the mountain in earlier seasons. Identification is being carried out with the help of the police forensic laboratory.

Every expedition is required to bring back a fixed amount of waste per climber and leaves a deposit that is refunded only when the rule is met. Officials admit enforcement above Camp Two has always been difficult because verification there is nearly impossible.

Nearly five hundred climbing permits were issued for the mountain this spring, close to the record set in recent years. Each permit brings a substantial royalty, and the money is meant to support mountain municipalities and cleanup work.

Guides say the crowding problem and the waste problem are the same problem. "More people on the same narrow route in the same two weeks means more of everything left behind," one veteran guide said.

A proposal under discussion would cap the number of permits per season and require every climber to carry a tracking device, but expedition operators have objected, saying it would push work to the other side of the mountain.
